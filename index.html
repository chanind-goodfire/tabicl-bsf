Sparse Dictionaries on TabICLv2

Training SAEs and block-sparse featurizers on a 27.6M-parameter tabular in-context learner, using its own synthetic training prior as the corpus.

11 November 2026390 dictionaries9 activation sites100M rows per site

Findings

  1. Read the ICL residual stream, as you guessed, with one twist at the end. The 512-d row vectors after each of the 12 in-context-learning blocks are the right target. In the last block only the test rows matter: train-row outputs feed nothing downstream, so final-layer dictionaries should be trained on test rows only.
  2. There is some superposition, but not much. Real activations are sparser than a Gaussian with the same covariance (+0.08 to +0.13 R² at k=8). But growing the dictionary from 1,024 to 65,536 latents adds at most 0.02 R² at any site. A dictionary 2–8× the residual width captures nearly everything.
  3. The largest feature family is a class-index code. At mid-depth about 830 of 2,260 frequent latents fire on “rows of class k”: train rows by their label, test rows by the model’s predicted class, through one shared code. The regressor has a counterpart: about 30% of its latents mark where a row’s target ranks within its table, mostly at the extremes.
  4. Those latents are causal. Adding one at 4× its mean activation turns 14–32% of test predictions to class k (random-latent controls: under 1%). Ablating all of them at block 11 costs 9 points of accuracy.
  5. BSFs trail SAEs at matched active dimensions and beat them at matched active units. Past 4,096 atoms the extra blocks die. One always-on block at block 11 combines a confidence axis shared by every table with a class layout that each table orients differently.

1Where to read the model

TabICLv2 takes a whole table at once: train rows with labels and test rows without. It predicts the test labels in one forward pass with no gradient updates. Each row passes through three stages, and each stage leaves a representation we can train a dictionary on.

Which rows to train on

Inside every ICL block the queries are all rows, but keys and values come only from train rows. Test rows read the context; no row ever reads a test row.

So in blocks 0–10 the train rows are the context that later blocks consult, and both kinds of row are worth modeling. In block 11 the train rows’ outputs go nowhere, because the head reads only test rows.

That gives a simple rule. Train on all rows at the row embedding and at ICL blocks 0–10, and on test rows only at block 11 and the post-LayerNorm head input. The head’s input is a fixed LayerNorm of the block-11 test rows, so the two final sites carry the same information. Training on train and test rows together at block 11 mixes in a distribution that never reaches the output. A test-row dictionary scored on train rows there drops from R² 0.96 to below zero (section 9).

The middle and final sites answer different questions. The middle layers show how the model builds its in-context computation; the final layer shows what the head reads. We trained both. The cell site (the column embedder’s 128-d output, sampled at 5% of cells) is easy to reconstruct, R² 0.99 at k=32, and we did not analyze it further.

2How much superposition?

A 512-d residual stream in a 27.6M-parameter model might not need superposition at all. Two baselines test that. PCA-k is the best rank-k linear reconstruction. The Gaussian null is an identical SAE trained on samples from a Gaussian with the same mean and covariance as the real activations. The null has the same PCA spectrum and no sparse structure, so any advantage of real over null is structure a sparse code can exploit.

Two results stand out. First, real activations do carry non-Gaussian structure. At k=8 the real-data SAE beats the null by 0.08 R² at the row embedding, 0.12 at ICL block 5 and 0.12 at the final layer.

Second, almost none of that structure needs a wide dictionary. On the Gaussian, width does nothing, as expected. On real data, width helps a little more but still barely: the largest gain from 1,024 to 16,384 or 65,536 latents anywhere in the sweep is 0.019 R² (block 5, k=8). In LLM SAEs the same change at fixed k is worth far more. A dictionary of roughly 1–4k latents in 512 dimensions has already found the directions the model uses. That is mild superposition: more features than dimensions, but not orders of magnitude more.

Correction to an earlier read. Before the null finished I called SAE ≫ PCA at matched dimension count (0.84 vs 0.58 at block 5, k=32) evidence of sparse structure. The Gaussian null alone reaches 0.76. Choosing the best 32 of many directions for each row beats a fixed 32-d subspace even on structureless data. So only the real-vs-null gap (0.08 there) counts as evidence.

The stream also uses most of its width. PCA needs 118 of 512 dimensions for 90% of variance at block 5 and 186 at block 7. Variance then concentrates toward the output: block-11 test rows need 35.

3The L0 × width sweep

Every site got two jobs that train many dictionaries in parallel on one shared, shuffled activation feed:

  • 13 BatchTopK SAEs: widths 1,024 to 65,536 at k = 8 to 64.
  • 24 BSFs: 4,096 or 16,384 atoms, group size 2/4/8, and an L0 grid, plus two dead-block fixes.

Each saw 100M rows from about 100k synthetic tables. The plot below puts SAEs and BSFs on one axis. Switch the x-axis to compare them per active dimension or per active unit (latent or block).

What the sweep says

  • Middle layers are hardest. SAE k=32 R² along depth (classifier): row 0.891 → block 2 0.870 → block 5 0.841 → block 8 0.854 → block 11 test rows 0.961. The regressor has the same U: 0.927 → 0.896 → 0.906.
  • BSF vs. SAE depends on what you hold fixed. At 32 active dimensions on block 5, SAE 0.841 beats BSF gs2 0.755, gs4 0.696 and gs8 0.655. At 8 active units, BSF gs4 (8 blocks of 4) reaches 0.696 against SAE k=8 at 0.615. Smaller groups are always closer to the SAE.
  • Atoms past 4,096 die. 16,384-atom BSFs match 4,096-atom BSFs to within 0.01 R² everywhere, with a median of 91% of blocks dead.
  • Dead blocks are mostly a row- and cell-site problem. At ICL blocks the base variant keeps 99–100% of blocks alive at the default shapes (gs2 × L0 16, gs4 × L0 8); only the lowest-L0 arms lose many (up to 82%). At the row embedding the same default shapes keep just 22–38%.
  • No dead-block fix wins everywhere. A reanimation loss brings gs4 and gs8 to ~100% alive at a cost of 0–0.02 R², but leaves gs2 at 43–81% alive. The “auto” variant (a stronger auxiliary loss and an automatic initial scale) keeps gs2 alive and fails at gs8 (5–26% alive).
  • 100M rows was more than enough. Every arm was within 0.01 R² of its final value after 25M rows.

5What the stream encodes

Before looking at features, linear probes show what is linearly available at each depth. The probes are logistic or ridge, cross-validated with folds split by table, on 32 rows from each of 1,500 held-out classifier tables.

  • In-context learning starts before the ICL blocks. The row embedding already predicts a test row’s true label at 0.66 (chance 0.21), because the column embedder sees train labels. Twelve ICL blocks raise that to only 0.79.
  • The model’s own answer becomes linear with depth. Its prediction goes from 0.71 to 0.97 accuracy; entropy from 0.61 to 0.95 R²; number of classes from 0.47 to 0.94.
  • Dataset shape fades. Feature count drops from 0.97 to 0.34 R² and train fraction from 0.73 to 0.30. Distance to the nearest neighbor is never linearly present (≤ 0.14).
  • Regressor. The test-row target rises from 0.51 to 0.73 R², the model’s predicted IQR (its uncertainty) from 0.67 to 0.87, and feature count fades from 0.95 to 0.43.

6A class-index code

We encoded 1.5M held-out rows with one k=32 SAE per site and correlated every frequent latent with row, table and prediction metadata. We then gave each latent a dominant role:

  • Class index: lift ≥ 3 for one class index, which holds at least half the latent’s mass.
  • Dataset-level: table identity explains ≥ 60% of the latent’s variance.
  • Metadata: the latent tracks one metadata variable at |r| ≥ 0.2.
  • No clear association: none of the above.

From block 2 on, 30–49% of frequent latents are class-index latents. Each fires on rows of one class index k: train rows by their label and test rows by the class the model predicts. Class indices are arbitrary per table, so the model is reusing one code for “class 3” across unrelated datasets. Its label embedding is shared across tables, so that code has to live somewhere.

  • The code is shared between train and test rows. 578–889 latents per site fire on train rows labeled k and on test rows predicted k.
  • It is not a proxy for other row properties. Its activations are not explained by number of classes, model confidence or kNN purity.
  • Within a class, firings are only mildly local. In the table’s own feature space, firing rows sit 14% closer to each other than random same-class rows (median distance ratio 0.86). A latent covers a broad part of a class, not one prototype.

The row embedding is different. Before any ICL block it has the most dataset-level latents (426 of 1,960), matching the probes: dataset shape is most present before the ICL blocks.

The regressor’s version: a target-rank code

The regressor has no classes, but it has a counterpart. Rank each row’s target within its own table: train rows by their label, test rows by where the model’s predicted median falls among the train targets. Then count latents that put at least half their activation on one window of two adjacent deciles (lift ≥ 2.5).

Roughly a quarter to a third of the regressor’s frequent latents are tuned to target rank. Most of them use the same window for train labels and test predictions, as the class-index code does. Most also sit at the ends of the range: they mark rows whose target is among the lowest or highest in their table rather than tiling the middle. A raw-target census missed all of this, because a target value means nothing across tables.

Steering them is less clean than steering class-index latents. We added single latents at 4× their mean activation on the test rows of 400 tables and measured the shift in the predicted median, in units of the table’s train-target standard deviation (medians over tables).

  • Block 11: 7 of 8 bottom-tuned latents lower the prediction (median shift −0.05, controls −0.004), but top-tuned latents barely raise it (+0.006).
  • Block 5: the effect runs backwards. Bottom-tuned latents raise the prediction (+0.24) and top-tuned ones lower it (−0.10).

So these latents carry target rank, but they are not simple dials for the output the way class-index latents are.

7Causal tests

We ran two interventions on test rows of 400 held-out tables. Steering adds one class-index latent’s decoder direction at α × its mean activation. Ablation subtracts the reconstructed contribution of every class-index latent at that site. Controls apply the same edit with latents that have no clear role.

Steering works at every depth tested and grows stronger toward the output: 14% of predictions move to the target class at block 2 and 32% at block 11. At α = 1, the effect is 1.6–5.8%. Ablation matters mostly at the end. At block 11 removing the class-index latents drops accuracy by 9.2 points while the control is flat. At block 2 the feature ablation (−1.3) is no worse than its control (−1.4). So this code drives the output from late layers, while earlier copies can be overwritten or rebuilt downstream.

8A block for confidence and class

In the block-11 BSF (gs4, L0 8, trained on all rows), block 1506 fires on 98% of rows across all 1,500 eval tables. Its 4-d code carries three things at once:

  • Confidence. A linear fit from three of its principal axes predicts the model’s top predicted probability on test rows with R² 0.62. Its activation grows as the model gets less sure (r = +0.54 with prediction entropy).
  • Class, laid out per table. Within a table, the code’s direction separates rows by class index (label for train rows, prediction for test rows). Class explains 79% of within-table direction variance in a split-half estimate. The layout rotates from table to table: pooled over all tables, class explains only 37%, and table identity alone explains 53%.
  • Task type. Binary tables form their own arm, away from multi-class tables.

So it is not a fixed class simplex. Each table lays its classes out in its own orientation, on top of a confidence axis that every table shares.

Every frequent block of this BSF and of the block-8 BSF can be browsed in the TabICL Block Explorer. It opens on block 1506 and shows the rows each block fires on inside their own tables, with the table-confound diagnostics for each block.

A retracted reading. A second block (1974) first looked like a class simplex, with its directions lined up by class. It fires in only 143 tables, and table identity explains 86% of its direction variance, so its class structure cannot be separated from which table a row came from. Any BSF block analysis on this model needs that table-identity control.

Correction (12 Nov). The first version of this section said class explains only 28% of block 1506’s direction variance within a table. That figure pooled class indices across tables. Measured within tables it is 79%.

9Negative results and pitfalls

  • No duplicate-row detector. The prior often repeats rows, so we looked for a latent that fires on exact duplicates of a train row. None separates them cleanly: AUROC is 0.53–0.66 within mixed tables, and dataset effects confound the larger numbers.
  • No convincing prototype picture. When we plotted a class-index latent’s firings in a table’s own feature space, they spread through the class rather than forming a cluster. That matches the 0.86 locality ratio, so the report shows the statistic instead of a hand-picked example.
  • Evaluate on the rows you trained on. Block-11 test-row SAEs looked broken on the full eval set, with R² 0.29–0.40 for wide arms. On test rows they score 0.92–0.975, matching training. Train rows at block 11 are a different distribution that the head never reads.
  • Precision. TabICL’s eval-mode inference autocasts to bf16, which corrupts predictions on raw prior tables. Run the model in training mode with dropout 0 in fp32. Leaving TF32 on keeps accuracy but shifts ~1% of rows’ activations by more than 10%.
  • Regressor metadata. Raw targets and linear baselines have extreme outliers (predictions up to ±2·10⁴). Clip before correlating with latents.

10Next steps

  • Label the rest. Run autointerp over top-activating rows for the 45–58% of latents with no clear association. Each row’s “context” is a whole table, so the explainer needs table summaries, not text snippets.
  • Check transfer to real data. Encode OpenML or TabArena tables with the prior-trained dictionaries and compare reconstruction and the class-index census.
  • Look across layers. The class-index code appears at every depth tested; a crosscoder or transcoder across ICL blocks would show whether it is one code carried forward or rebuilt.
  • Study the regressor. Work out why steering block-5 target-rank latents moves predictions the wrong way; one possibility is that the model compares each row with its context rather than reading an absolute rank. Its final layer is very low-rank (participation ratio 1.9 at the head input), so a small dictionary over block-11 test rows may map out its quantile computation completely.

Train rows / test rows
Rows with labels given in the context, and rows the model must predict. Train fraction is 0.3–0.9 per table.
R² / FVU
Fraction of variance explained by a reconstruction, and its complement (fraction of variance unexplained), pooled over held-out rows.
L0, k
Number of active latents per row. BatchTopK fixes the average across a batch at k.
AuxK
An auxiliary loss that lets dead latents reconstruct the residual error, so they get gradient again.
BSF
Block-sparse featurizer: the dictionary is split into blocks of gs atoms. A top-k gate picks L0 blocks per row, and each active block gets a gs-dimensional signed code.
Atoms
Total dictionary rows in a BSF, equal to blocks × group size.
Dead latent / block
One that never fires on the eval set.
Gaussian null
A dictionary trained on Gaussian samples with the real activations’ mean and covariance.
PCA-k
Reconstruction from the top k principal components.
Class-index latent
A latent concentrated on one class index k: train rows labeled k or test rows predicted k.
Lift
A latent’s activation share on one class divided by that class’s base rate.
Prior
The synthetic table generator TabICLv2 was trained on: random structural causal models over 1–100 features, up to 1,024 rows per table.

BMethods and files

  • Model. TabICLv2 classifier and regressor checkpoints, 27.55M parameters each, run in fp32.
  • Corpus. 2 × 196,608 tables harvested from the stage-1 prior (seeds disjoint from the eval set). A seeded replay gives every job the same table stream. Activations are computed on the fly and mixed in a 1,048,576-row buffer.
  • Eval set. 1,500 tables per model with per-row metadata (label, model prediction and confidence, kNN and linear baselines, duplicates, outlierness) and per-table metadata. Sweep R² uses a fixed 300-table subset.
  • Training. One job trains 13 SAE or 24 BSF arms in parallel on one GPU from a shared activation stream, ~1.5–3 h per job.
